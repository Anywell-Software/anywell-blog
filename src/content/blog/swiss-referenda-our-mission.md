Last Sunday, Switzerland voted again. Federal initiatives, cantonal and communal proposals and results that made headlines for a few hours. What made fewer headlines: more than half of the people allowed to vote did not. They let someone else decide.

This is not an exception. It is the norm. And the group that stays home most often is young adults.

## A democracy that asks a lot

Switzerland is a direct democracy by design. Several times a year, citizens vote on concrete proposals at the federal, cantonal and communal level. A typical voting Sunday brings a few federal proposals, some cantonal ones and maybe one from your municipality. Over a year, that adds up to a long list of decisions that change laws, budgets or even the constitution.

That is remarkable. Very few countries give their citizens this much direct influence. And yet, vote after vote, only around half of the electorate actually takes part. The last voting Sunday was no exception.

## Who stays home?

Switzerland has no national statistic on turnout by age. But some places track it, like the Canton of St. Gallen and the picture is clear: young adults vote far less often than older generations. At some elections, older people are almost twice as likely to show up.

The interesting part: young people are not simply apathetic. They vote selectively. When a topic directly affects them, like pensions, turnout among young adults jumps noticeably. A few weeks later, on topics that feel far away, it drops again.

So when young people understand what is at stake and feel it concerns them, they show up. The real question is why that happens so rarely.

## Two words: infobesity and political illiteracy

Over the past months, we talked to people around us and dug through studies and statistics. Two ideas kept coming up.

### Infobesity

If you have ever opened a Swiss voting envelope, you know what we mean. On a busy Sunday, you may be asked to decide on federal, cantonal and communal proposals at the same time, each about a completely different topic. Foreign policy on one page, agriculture on the next, a school renovation on the third. Some proposals come with a counter-proposal and a tie-break question, which adds yet another layer to understand.

To explain all of this, there are voting booklets. Not just one, but one for each level: federal, cantonal and communal. Together they can easily fill an evening of reading.

And the booklets are only the starting point. To really understand the consequences of a decision, you need debates, newspaper analysis and sometimes expert studies. Meanwhile, campaigns often rely on emotional messages designed to persuade rather than to inform.

The problem is not a lack of information. It is too much of it, with no easy way to tell what actually matters. For many people, the most rational reaction is not to start at all.

### Political illiteracy

The second problem is more basic. Many people simply do not know how Swiss democracy works. What is the difference between an initiative and a referendum? Why do some proposals need a majority of both the people and the cantons? How do you start one, or sign one?

Most of us learned this once in school, if at all, and forgot it soon after. And when you do not understand the rules, the game starts to feel like it was made for someone else. That is exactly how many young people describe politics: something for older, well-informed people. Not for them.

## Our answer: AnyWell

We believe both problems can be tackled the same way: by making political knowledge small, clear and a little bit fun. And by giving people a place where politics does not stop at learning, but turns into conversation.

That is why we are building **AnyWell**, a civic education app about Swiss democracy.

### The idea

Nobody learns a language by reading a thick grammar book in one evening. You learn it in small steps, a few minutes a day, with instant feedback and a reason to come back tomorrow. We think political knowledge works the same way.

But knowing how the system works is only the first step. Democracy lives from people talking to each other, disagreeing, and finding out where they stand. So AnyWell is built around two things: **learning** and **taking part**.

### Learn

**Pick a topic.** Lessons are grouped into topics, for example:

- *Voting basics:* How does a voting Sunday work? What happens to your envelope?
- *Institutions:* Who are the Federal Council, the National Council and the Council of States, and what do they actually do?
- *Your rights:* What is the difference between an initiative and a referendum? How do you start or sign one?
- *Elections:* How are parliament seats distributed, and why does your canton matter?

**Start a lesson.** Each lesson is a short series of questions, from beginner to advanced. Single choice, multiple choice or true or false. A lesson takes a few minutes, not an evening.

**Learn from every answer.** After each question you immediately see whether you were right, together with a short explanation. The goal is not to test you. It is to make sure you leave every lesson knowing a bit more than before.

**Keep going.** You earn XP for every lesson and build a streak for every day you learn. Small rewards, but they help turn "I should really understand this" into a habit.

### Take part

**Share your opinion.** Once you understand a topic, you can say what you think about it. Agree, disagree, explain your view and see how others see it. Politics feels a lot less distant when you realise people your age are discussing the same questions.

**Find your parties.** Not sure which party actually represents what you believe? On AnyWell you can explore the parties, see what they stand for and choose the ones that fit you. Not because an app tells you to, but because you compared them yourself.

**Meet the politicians.** Politicians can join the platform too. That means you do not just read about them in the newspaper. You can see their positions, follow them and engage with them directly. Politics stops being something that happens "up there" and becomes something you can talk to.

### Neutral by design

AnyWell itself does not tell you how to vote. Our lessons explain how the system works and what is at stake, without taking sides. Opinions on AnyWell come from its users and from the politicians on the platform, and every party is welcome. Our job is to provide the stage, not the script.

We are also not a company. AnyWell is a leisure project, built by a small team of students in our spare time, because we want our own generation to feel at home in Swiss democracy.

And since Switzerland speaks more than one language, so does AnyWell: German, French, Italian, Romansh and English.

### Where we are

The app is still in development and not launched yet. We will share our progress, the ideas behind it and what we learn along the way right here on the blog. So stay tuned.

Switzerland does not need more democracy. It needs more people who feel ready to use the one it already has.
